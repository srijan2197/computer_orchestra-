================================================================================
 1. PROBLEM UNDERSTANDING
================================================================================

1. What problem are you solving?
   Modern vehicles are consolidating dozens of applications (ADAS, cockpit,
   infotainment, connectivity) onto ONE shared high-performance compute (HPC)
   platform. These apps have mixed criticality: safety-critical (ASIL) apps must
   never miss deadlines, while best-effort (QM) apps like media/OTA compete for
   the same CPU/GPU/NPU. We built an orchestration layer that dynamically and
   safely shares compute so ASIL workloads are always protected while overall
   hardware utilisation stays high.

2. Why is static resource allocation inefficient?
   Static allocation hard-partitions the SoC (e.g. fixed core/GPU slices per app)
   sized for each app's WORST case. Most of the time apps run below worst case,
   so reserved-but-idle capacity is wasted. It also can't adapt: when a scenario
   changes (e.g. emergency braking), a statically-capped safety app can't borrow
   the idle capacity it suddenly needs. You over-provision hardware AND still risk
   deadline misses.

3. What is a cross-domain software orchestration layer?
   A software control plane that sits between the vehicle applications and the
   shared silicon. It continuously measures each workload's demand, applies a
   safety-aware scheduling policy, and allocates CPU/GPU/NPU across domains
   (ADAS, cockpit, infotainment...) in real time — enforcing freedom-from-
   interference between safety levels while maximising utilisation.

4. Why are centralized vehicle compute platforms becoming popular?
   Fewer, more powerful compute units replace 100+ distributed ECUs, reducing
   wiring, weight, cost and complexity. They enable Software-Defined Vehicles:
   features become software you can update over-the-air, and spare compute can be
   monetised. But consolidation creates the mixed-criticality sharing problem this
   project solves.

5. What challenges arise when multiple applications share the same hardware?
   Resource contention (two apps want the same GPU), interference (a greedy QM app
   starving a safety app), latency/jitter spikes, deadline misses, and safety
   certification difficulty (you must prove the infotainment app can't affect
   emergency braking). Our layer addresses all of these via priority ordering plus
   an ASIL capacity reserve.

6. Why is dynamic resource allocation needed?
   Vehicle load is highly non-stationary — it depends on driving scenario (city,
   highway, parking, emergency). Emergency braking needs a burst of NPU/CPU
   instantly; parking needs GPU for surround-view. Dynamic allocation shifts
   capacity to where it's needed each tick, giving both high utilisation and
   deadline safety that static schemes cannot.

7. What happens when compute resources become overloaded?
   Our scheduler degrades gracefully: it serves workloads in strict safety-then-
   priority order, so ASIL apps get full allocation first. QM apps are THROTTLED
   (given a reduced service ratio), not the safety apps. The simulator raises a
   "<resource> saturated ... QM workloads throttled" alert and marks safety status
   DEGRADED (QM missed a deadline) rather than VIOLATION (ASIL protected).

8. How does your solution improve resource utilisation?
   Instead of reserving worst-case slices, we pool all capacity and greedily hand
   it out every tick based on live demand. Idle capacity from one domain is
   immediately reused by another (subject to the ASIL reserve), so the platform
   runs closer to its true limit while still guaranteeing safety headroom.

9. Why can't traditional scheduling mechanisms solve this problem?
   Classic OS schedulers (Round Robin, CFS) are fairness/throughput oriented and
   criticality-agnostic — they can let a QM app steal time from an ASIL app. Pure
   real-time schedulers (RMS/EDF) handle deadlines on ONE CPU but don't reason
   about heterogeneous CPU+GPU+NPU pools, cross-domain freedom-from-interference,
   or scenario-driven demand. We combine safety weighting + priority + deadline
   urgency across all three resource pools.

10. What are the limitations of your approach?
    It is a simulation/orchestration-policy prototype: it models demand and uses a
    queueing latency model rather than running real containers on real silicon. It
    assumes demand is measurable and the platform is trusted. The AI predictor is a
    lightweight statistical model (not a deep net). In production you'd bind it to a
    real hypervisor/cgroups enforcement layer and certified timing analysis.

 2. AUTOMOTIVE DOMAIN
================================================================================

SDV & Vehicle Architecture

1. What is a Software Defined Vehicle (SDV)?
   A vehicle whose features are primarily defined and upgraded through software on
   centralised compute, decoupled from fixed hardware. Functions can be added,
   improved, or monetised via OTA updates over the vehicle's life.

2. What is a domain controller?
   A powerful ECU that consolidates a functional domain (e.g. ADAS, cockpit, body)
   that used to be many small ECUs. Our catalog mirrors this — workloads are tagged
   by domain: ADAS, Cockpit, Infotainment, Connectivity.

3. What is a zonal architecture?
   Compute organised by physical ZONE of the car (front-left, rear...) rather than
   by function. Zone controllers aggregate local sensors/actuators and connect to
   central HPCs over high-speed Ethernet, cutting wiring. Our shared SoC represents
   such a central HPC.

4. What is centralized compute architecture?
   A few high-performance compute units run most vehicle software, versus dozens of
   distributed single-function ECUs. This is exactly the shared platform our
   orchestrator manages (CPU=900, GPU=800, NPU=1200 CU).

5. Why are vehicles moving from ECU-based systems to centralized compute?
   Cost/weight/wiring reduction, easier OTA updates, more compute for AI/ADAS,
   simpler integration, and the ability to reallocate spare compute. The trade-off
   is the mixed-criticality sharing challenge we solve.

6. How many applications can run on a modern vehicle computer?
   Dozens to hundreds. Our demo runs 12 representative workloads spanning all major
   domains, which is enough to demonstrate contention and safety isolation.

7. What vehicle domains are involved in your solution?
   - ADAS: AEB, ACC, Lane Keeping, Sensor Fusion, Surround-View/Parking
   - Cockpit: Driver Monitoring, Digital Instrument Cluster
   - Infotainment: Navigation, Media, Voice Assistant, Connected Apps
   - Connectivity: OTA Update Agent
   These map to Autonomous/ADAS, Infotainment, Telematics/Connectivity and Body/
   Cockpit categories.


================================================================================
 3. FUNCTIONAL SAFETY (ISO 26262)
================================================================================

1. What is ISO 26262?
   The international standard for functional safety of electrical/electronic
   systems in road vehicles. It defines a safety lifecycle and the ASIL risk-
   classification scheme.

2. What is functional safety?
   Freedom from unacceptable risk due to malfunctioning E/E systems — ensuring the
   system behaves safely even when faults occur.

3. What is ASIL?
   Automotive Safety Integrity Level — a hazard classification (A to D) derived from
   Severity x Exposure x Controllability. Higher ASIL = stricter safety
   requirements. In code, models.py maps ASIL to a numeric weight (A=1...D=4, QM=0)
   used by the scheduler.

4. Difference between ASIL and QM?
   QM (Quality Managed) items carry no ASIL safety requirement — standard quality
   processes suffice (e.g. infotainment). ASIL items require dedicated safety
   measures. In our system is_safety_critical is True for A-D and False for QM.

5. What are ASIL-A, B, C, D?
   Increasing integrity levels. A = lowest safety requirement, D = highest/most
   stringent. Our workloads: AEB=D, ACC=C, LKA=C, Fusion=B, DMS=B, Surround=B,
   Cluster=A; all infotainment/OTA = QM.

6. Which is the highest safety level?
   ASIL-D.

7. Why is emergency braking ASIL-D?
   A failure can directly cause a high-severity collision, the driver has low
   controllability, and exposure is common — that combination yields ASIL-D. In
   catalog.py AEB is asil="D", priority=10, deadline_ms=10 (tightest deadline).

8. Why is infotainment classified as QM?
   Its malfunction (media stutter, nav delay) does not cause direct physical harm,
   so it needs no ASIL rating. In catalog media/nav/voice/appstore/OTA are QM.

9. What happens if QM workloads affect ASIL workloads?
   That would be a freedom-from-interference violation. We prevent it: ASIL always
   ordered ahead of QM (effective_priority weights ASIL x 10,000) AND a 70% reserve
   band per resource. If ASIL ever missed a deadline the dashboard shows safety
   status VIOLATION — by design QM overload only produces DEGRADED (QM throttled).

10. How do you ensure safety isolation?
    Two mechanisms in scheduler.py: (a) strict priority ordering that serves all
    safety-critical demand before any QM demand; (b) an asil_reserve fraction (0.7)
    that guarantees a protected capacity band for the ASIL domain so QM can never
    consume it. The metrics layer independently verifies asil_all_met each tick.


================================================================================
 4. SCHEDULER DESIGN
================================================================================

1. How does your scheduler work?
   Each tick: compute every workload's live demand, sort all active workloads by
   effective_priority (safety weight, then base priority, then deadline urgency),
   then greedily allocate each resource (CPU/GPU/NPU) down that ordered list until
   capacity is exhausted. Then derive each workload's service ratio, latency and
   deadline compliance. See scheduler.py: schedule().

2. What inputs are considered by the scheduler?
   Per-workload instantaneous demand per resource, ASIL level (asil_weight), base
   priority (1-10), deadline_ms, enabled flag, the platform capacities, and the
   asil_reserve fraction.

3. How are priorities assigned?
   effective_priority = asil_weight*10,000 + priority*100 + deadline_urgency,
   where deadline_urgency = 1000/deadline_ms. Safety dominates, then configured
   priority, then how tight the deadline is.

4. How do you classify workloads?
   By domain (ADAS/Cockpit/Infotainment/Connectivity) and by criticality
   (is_safety_critical = ASIL A-D vs QM). Each has a demand vector and a per-
   scenario intensity multiplier.

5. How do you allocate CPU resources?
   Greedy priority-ordered allocation: _allocate_resource("CPU", 900, ordered)
   grants each workload min(its CPU demand, remaining CPU) walking down the sorted
   list, so highest-criticality gets served first.

6. How do you allocate GPU resources?
   Identically, over the 800 CU GPU pool. GPU-heavy apps (surround-view, cluster,
   media, lane-keeping) contend here and are served in safety-priority order.

7. How do you allocate NPU resources?
   Identically, over the 1200 CU NPU pool — this is where AI/inference apps (AEB,
   ACC, fusion, DMS, voice) compete; safety inference is served first.

8. What triggers resource reallocation?
   Every tick (1 Hz). There is no incremental patching — the full allocation is
   recomputed from current demand, scenario, spikes, and toggles, so it adapts
   instantly to any change.

9. How often does scheduling occur?
   Once per simulation tick = every 1 second (SIM_INTERVAL = 1.0 in main.py). The
   design supports a faster tick; 1 Hz is chosen for a readable live dashboard.

10. Is your scheduler preemptive or non-preemptive?
    Conceptually preemptive/priority-based: because allocation is fully recomputed
    each tick, a newly urgent ASIL app immediately reclaims capacity from QM apps
    (which are throttled), equivalent to preempting their share.


================================================================================
 5. SCHEDULING ALGORITHM
================================================================================

1. Which scheduling algorithm did you use?
   A custom adaptive, safety-aware, priority-greedy allocator: strict criticality
   ordering + weighted priority + deadline-urgency tie-break + an ASIL reserve
   band. It is a mixed-criticality static-priority scheme with graceful QM
   degradation.

2. Why did you choose it?
   It directly guarantees freedom-from-interference (the #1 ISO 26262 concern),
   is O(n log n) and deterministic (predictable for real-time), handles
   heterogeneous CPU/GPU/NPU pools together, and degrades QM gracefully instead of
   dropping safety tasks.

3. Did you consider Round Robin?
   Yes — rejected. RR is fairness-oriented and criticality-blind; it would give an
   infotainment app equal turns against emergency braking, risking safety deadline
   misses.

4. Did you consider Earliest Deadline First?
   Yes. EDF is optimal for single-resource deadline scheduling, but on an
   overloaded multi-resource platform EDF can cause a cascade of misses and doesn't
   encode safety criticality. We fold deadline urgency into our priority function
   instead, while keeping ASIL protection dominant.

5. Did you consider Rate Monotonic Scheduling?
   Yes. RMS assigns priority by period and is great for fixed periodic tasks on one
   CPU, but it's criticality-agnostic and single-resource. Our demand is scenario-
   driven and spans three resource pools, so pure RMS didn't fit.

6. Did you consider Weighted Fair Scheduling?
   Yes — our reserve band is inspired by it. But pure WFS shares proportionally even
   under overload, which could still let QM erode ASIL. We enforce hard safety
   ordering first, then behave fair-share-like within the remaining capacity.

7. What are the tradeoffs?
   We favour safety and determinism over strict fairness and over theoretical
   single-CPU optimality. Low-priority QM apps can be heavily throttled under load
   (acceptable — they're non-safety). We recompute globally each tick (simple,
   predictable) rather than doing incremental micro-optimisation.

8. How do you handle starvation?
   ASIL apps can never be starved (reserve + ordering). For QM, starvation is
   bounded because demand fluctuates and low-priority apps still receive whatever
   capacity remains; the dashboard surfaces throttled_count so operators see it.
   The reserve caps how much ASIL can pre-empt, leaving room for QM when possible.

9. How do you handle priority inversion?
   There is no lock-based blocking between workloads — allocation is a stateless
   per-tick computation, so classic priority inversion cannot occur. A high-
   priority app never waits on a resource held by a lower-priority one.

10. How do you handle deadline misses?
    We compute latency_ms = base_latency_ms / service_ratio and flag
    deadline_met = latency <= deadline_ms. Misses are counted (deadline_misses)
    and drive safety_status: VIOLATION if any ASIL misses, DEGRADED if only QM
    misses, NOMINAL otherwise. Alerts are emitted for the operator.


================================================================================
 6. RESOURCE MANAGEMENT
================================================================================

CPU
1. How is CPU utilisation measured?
   summary["CPU"]["util"] = used / capacity, where used is the sum of CPU CU
   granted this tick and capacity = 900. Shown as a percentage on the dashboard.

2. How do you reserve CPU cores?
   Via asil_reserve (default 0.7): reserved_asil = capacity * reserve = 630 CU of
   CPU is treated as guaranteed for the ASIL domain, reported per resource and
   protected by priority ordering.

3. What happens if CPU reaches 100% utilisation?
   summary marks saturated=True; QM apps get reduced service ratios (throttled),
   their latency rises, and an alert "CPU saturated ... QM workloads throttled"
   fires. ASIL apps remain fully served due to ordering + reserve.

GPU
1. How is GPU sharing performed?
   The 800 CU GPU pool is allocated greedily in safety-priority order among GPU
   consumers (cluster, surround-view, media, lane-keeping, fusion, etc.).

2. How do you prevent GPU contention?
   By ordering: safety GPU users are served before QM GPU users, and the reserve
   keeps a protected band. Under contention QM GPU apps (media) are throttled first.

3. What happens when multiple AI workloads need the GPU/accelerator?
   They queue by effective priority; the highest-criticality inference gets full
   allocation, lower ones share the remainder. If demand exceeds capacity, QM
   inference is throttled and forecast/saturation alerts are raised.

NPU
1. What is an NPU?
   Neural Processing Unit — a dedicated accelerator for neural-network inference
   (matrix/MAC operations), far more efficient than CPU/GPU for AI workloads.

2. Why use an NPU instead of a CPU?
   Much higher performance-per-watt and lower latency for inference. Our largest
   pool is NPU (1200 CU) because ADAS perception (AEB, fusion, ACC) and DMS/voice
   are inference-heavy.

3. Which workloads run on an NPU?
   AEB (180), ACC (120), Lane Keeping (140), Sensor Fusion (160), DMS (150),
   Surround (100), Voice (130), Connected Apps (20) — see demand vectors in
   catalog.py.

Memory
1. How do you manage memory contention?
   In this prototype the three modelled pools are CPU/GPU/NPU compute; memory is
   abstracted into the same Compute-Unit demand model. The identical greedy-with-
   reserve policy generalises to a memory-bandwidth pool by adding it to PLATFORM.

2. How do you avoid memory exhaustion?
   Same mechanism: cap total granted CU at capacity and reserve a safety band, so
   the safety domain always keeps its guaranteed share and QM is throttled before
   exhaustion harms ASIL.


================================================================================
 7. SAFETY PARTITIONING
================================================================================

1. How do you isolate ASIL workloads?
   ASIL workloads are always ordered ahead of QM (asil_weight x 10,000) and a
   per-resource reserve band (70%) is guaranteed to them, so their allocation is
   never reduced by QM demand.

2. How do you isolate QM workloads?
   QM apps only ever receive capacity left after safety demand is met; they operate
   in the non-reserved band and are the first to be throttled under pressure.

3. Can QM workloads access ASIL resources?
   No. The reserved ASIL band and priority ordering ensure QM cannot consume the
   protected safety capacity.

4. What happens if QM exceeds allocated resources?
   Its service_ratio drops below 1, latency inflates, it is flagged throttled and
   may miss its (loose) deadline -> safety_status DEGRADED, never VIOLATION.

5. How are resource quotas enforced?
   By the scheduler's greedy allocation limited to each resource's capacity and by
   the asil_reserve fraction. Allocation is recomputed and enforced every tick.

6. Do you use containers?
   Not in the prototype — it's a policy/orchestration engine. The design maps
   cleanly onto containers/cgroups: each workload's allocated CU becomes a cgroup
   CPU/GPU quota in production.

7. Do you use virtual machines?
   No VMs in the demo. In production the enforcement layer would typically be a
   safety-certified hypervisor (e.g. partitioning per ISO 26262 / AUTOSAR Adaptive)
   consuming our allocation decisions.

8. How do you guarantee safety?
   Deterministic priority ordering + reserved capacity band + per-tick verification
   that asil_all_met. If ASIL is ever unsatisfiable the system flags CRITICAL,
   making the guarantee observable and testable.

9. Can ASIL workloads borrow resources from QM?
   Yes — that's the whole point of dynamic sharing. When ASIL demand spikes (e.g.
   emergency), it reclaims capacity that QM was using; QM is throttled to make room.

10. Can QM workloads borrow resources from ASIL?
    Only capacity outside the reserved ASIL band and only what safety apps aren't
    currently using — and they are pre-empted the instant safety demand returns.
    QM can never intrude on the guaranteed ASIL reserve.


================================================================================
 8. REAL-TIME SYSTEMS
================================================================================

1. What is a real-time system?
   A system whose correctness depends on producing results within defined time
   bounds, not just logically correct outputs.

2. What is a hard real-time task?
   A task where missing a deadline is a failure/hazard (e.g. emergency braking).

3. What is a soft real-time task?
   A task where an occasional late result degrades quality but isn't catastrophic
   (e.g. navigation, media).

4. Give examples of hard real-time applications.
   AEB (deadline 10 ms), ACC (20 ms), Lane Keeping (30 ms), Sensor Fusion (30 ms),
   Instrument Cluster (16 ms) — all short deadlines in catalog.py.

5. What is a deadline miss?
   When a workload's response time exceeds its deadline_ms. We detect it via
   latency_ms > deadline_ms and count/flag it.

6. How do you reduce latency?
   By guaranteeing high service ratios to critical apps (full allocation ->
   service_ratio 1 -> latency = base_latency_ms). Throttling is pushed onto QM so
   safety latency stays minimal.

7. What is jitter?
   Variation in response time / period between successive executions. Consistent
   allocation each tick keeps safety-app latency stable, minimising jitter.

8. How does jitter affect ADAS?
   ADAS control loops assume regular timing; jitter degrades perception/control
   stability and can cause unsafe behaviour. Protecting ASIL allocation keeps their
   timing (and thus jitter) tight.

9. What response time is acceptable?
   Per-workload, defined by deadline_ms: AEB 10 ms, ACC 20 ms, cluster 16 ms, up to
   QM apps at 100-1000 ms. Acceptable = latency within that deadline.

10. How do you measure latency?
    Queueing model: latency_ms = base_latency_ms / max(service_ratio, 0.05). Full
    service = base latency; degraded service inflates latency proportionally.


================================================================================
 9. MONITORING
================================================================================

1. How do you monitor CPU? 2. GPU? 3. Memory?
   Every tick the simulator sums granted CU per resource and computes util,
   asil_used, qm_used, demand and saturation flags in the summary object — streamed
   to the dashboard for CPU, GPU and NPU (memory generalises the same way).

4. How do you collect metrics?
   simulator.step() builds a metrics block: avg_latency_ms, deadline_misses,
   throttled_count, asil_all_met, safety_status, active_workloads, total_util.

5. How frequently are metrics collected?
   Every tick — 1 Hz (SIM_INTERVAL = 1.0s) — and pushed live over WebSocket.

6. How do you detect overload?
   Per resource, saturated = used >= capacity. Plus predictive detection:
   predicted_util > 0.95 flags predict_saturation before it actually happens.

7. How do you generate alerts?
   The snapshot builds an alerts list: saturation ("<res> saturated ... throttled"),
   AI forecast warnings ("approaching saturation"), and CRITICAL ASIL-exceeds-
   capacity messages. The frontend AlertsBar renders them.

8. What KPIs are displayed?
   Per-resource utilisation, ASIL vs QM usage split, average latency, deadline
   misses, throttled count, overall safety status, total platform utilisation, and
   predicted demand/utilisation.

9. How do you calculate utilisation?
   Per resource: used/capacity. Overall total_util = sum(used) / sum(capacity)
   across CPU+GPU+NPU.

10. How is performance visualised?
    React + Recharts dashboard: ResourceCards, UtilizationChart, PredictionChart,
    MetricsSummary, WorkloadTable, SafetyPartition view and AlertsBar — all updating
    live from the WebSocket stream.

10. Can users simulate workload changes?
    Yes — ControlPanel lets you switch driving scenario, enable/disable workloads,
    inject demand spikes, adjust the ASIL reserve and pause/resume the simulation.


================================================================================
 11. AI PREDICTION (STRETCH GOAL)
================================================================================

1. Why use AI in scheduling?
   To act pre-emptively: forecasting the next tick's demand lets the orchestrator
   reserve headroom BEFORE a spike causes deadline misses, instead of reacting after
   the fact.

2. What data is used for prediction?
   The per-tick total observed demand per resource (CPU/GPU/NPU), stored in a
   rolling 40-sample history.

3. Which ML model did you use?
   Holt's linear-trend method = double exponential smoothing (a level term + a
   trend term). See predictor.py DemandPredictor.

4. Why did you choose that model?
   It is dependency-free pure Python, O(1) per update, runs in real time on an
   embedded target, captures both current level and rising/falling trend, and its
   interface can be swapped for an LSTM/Temporal-CNN without changing the system.

5. What features are used?
   The smoothed level and trend of each resource's aggregate demand time series
   (alpha=0.4 level smoothing, beta=0.3 trend smoothing).

6. How do you train the model?
   It learns online — no offline training. Each tick update() adjusts level and
   trend from the newest observation; it self-adapts to the current driving regime.

7. What is workload prediction?
   Forecasting near-future compute demand so the scheduler can anticipate
   saturation and protect safety headroom proactively.

8. How accurate is your prediction?
   Reported per resource via a confidence score derived from recent relative
   variance (confidence()): stable demand -> high confidence, volatile -> lower. The
   PredictionChart overlays forecast vs actual so accuracy is visible.

9. What happens when prediction is wrong?
   Nothing unsafe — prediction is advisory (early-warning alerts only). Actual
   allocation each tick is still driven by REAL measured demand with the hard safety
   guarantees, so a bad forecast can't cause a safety violation.

10. How does prediction improve performance?
    Early saturation warnings (predicted_util > 0.95) let operators/policy shed or
    defer QM load before a spike, reducing reactive deadline misses and smoothing
    utilisation.


================================================================================
 12. ARCHITECTURE
================================================================================

1. Explain your architecture.
   Three layers: (a) Backend engine — Simulator drives demand, Scheduler allocates,
   Predictor forecasts, over shared models/catalog; (b) API — FastAPI with a 1 Hz
   background loop broadcasting JSON snapshots over WebSocket /ws (+ REST fallback);
   (c) Frontend — React/Vite dashboard consuming the stream and sending control
   messages.

2. What are the major components?
   models.py (data), catalog.py (platform+workloads), scheduler.py (allocation),
   predictor.py (AI), simulator.py (tick engine), main.py (API), and the React
   components under frontend/src.

3. Why is orchestration separated from applications?
   Separation of concerns: apps declare demand; the orchestrator owns allocation
   policy and safety enforcement centrally. This is what makes freedom-from-
   interference provable and the policy reusable across apps.

4. How do modules communicate?
   In-process function calls in the backend (simulator -> scheduler -> predictor);
   backend-to-frontend via WebSocket JSON snapshots; frontend-to-backend via
   WebSocket/REST control messages.

5. How do you scale the solution?
   Stateless per-tick scheduling and a clean interface let you add resources to
   PLATFORM, add workloads to the catalog, raise the tick rate, or replace the
   predictor — without touching the core loop.

6. Is the architecture modular?
   Yes — each concern is its own module with narrow interfaces (e.g. schedule()
   takes workloads+platform+reserve and returns a summary), so components are
   independently replaceable.

7. Which component makes decisions?
   scheduler.py (schedule / effective_priority / _allocate_resource).

8. Which component collects metrics?
   simulator.py (_build_snapshot assembles resources, metrics and alerts).

9. Which component enforces safety?
   scheduler.py via priority ordering + asil_reserve; simulator verifies
   asil_all_met and sets safety_status.

10. Which component handles prediction?
    predictor.py (DemandPredictor).


================================================================================
 13. DESIGN DECISIONS
================================================================================

1. Why did you choose this architecture?
   A clean split between a deterministic policy core and a live visualisation makes
   the safety behaviour easy to reason about, test and demo, while keeping each part
   swappable.

2. Why did you use microservices?
   We deliberately did NOT — for a real-time control loop, in-process calls avoid
   network latency/jitter. Backend and frontend are separate processes, which is
   enough separation here.

3. Why did you use a monolithic (single-process) backend?
   The scheduler must run deterministically at a fixed cadence; a single-process
   engine gives predictable timing with no inter-service overhead — appropriate for
   an embedded orchestration layer.

4. Why React?
   Component model fits a live dashboard, huge ecosystem (Recharts for charts,
   lucide-react for icons), and efficient re-rendering for streaming data.

5. Why FastAPI?
   Async-first (ideal for WebSocket streaming + background tasks), automatic
   validation via Pydantic, minimal boilerplate, and high performance.

6. Why PostgreSQL?
   Not used in the demo (state is live/in-memory). If persistence were needed
   (historical metrics, audit of safety events), PostgreSQL is the natural choice
   for reliable relational time-series/event storage.

7. Why WebSockets?
   The dashboard needs low-latency push of 1 Hz snapshots; WebSockets give
   bidirectional streaming (server pushes state, client sends controls) without
   polling overhead. REST endpoints exist as a fallback.

8. Why not Kubernetes?
   K8s orchestrates cloud containers at second/minute granularity — the wrong tool
   for millisecond in-vehicle real-time scheduling. Our layer is the vehicle-side
   equivalent; K8s could manage a cloud/backoffice twin, not the safety loop.

9. What would you change in production?
   Bind allocations to a real enforcement layer (certified hypervisor / cgroups /
   AUTOSAR Adaptive), add persistence + auth, run at higher tick rate, replace the
   predictor with a trained model, and add formal timing/WCET analysis.

10. What are the design tradeoffs?
    Simplicity/determinism/safety over micro-optimised fairness; simulation over
    real silicon enforcement; a lightweight statistical predictor over a heavy ML
    model. These keep the safety story clear and the demo real-time.


================================================================================
 14. SCENARIO-BASED
================================================================================

Scenario 1 — Emergency braking starts suddenly (scenario="emergency")
1. What happens in your system?
   AEB intensity jumps to 2.0x (plus other ADAS rise); its high ASIL-D effective
   priority means it's allocated first, reclaiming CPU/NPU from QM apps, which are
   throttled. Safety status stays NOMINAL for ASIL.
2. How quickly are resources reallocated?
   Within one tick — allocation is fully recomputed each cycle, so the shift is
   immediate on the next scheduling pass.
3. Which workloads are reduced first?
   Lowest effective priority first: QM apps — Connected Apps, OTA, Media, Voice,
   Navigation — before any ASIL app is touched.

Scenario 2 — Voice assistant consumes 90% GPU/accelerator
1. How does the system react?
   Voice is QM (priority 4); it only holds capacity ASIL apps aren't using. As ADAS
   demand rises, voice is throttled back down its service ratio.
2. Does it impact ADAS?
   No — ASIL ordering + the 70% reserve guarantee ADAS gets its capacity first; the
   scheduler pre-empts the QM voice app.
3. How do you prevent unsafe behaviour?
   Priority ordering + reserved ASIL band; the metrics layer confirms asil_all_met
   and would flag VIOLATION if safety were ever affected (it isn't).

Scenario 3 — CPU utilisation reaches 100%
1. How does your scheduler respond?
   summary marks CPU saturated; safety apps keep full CPU, QM apps get reduced
   service ratios (throttled), and a saturation alert is raised.
2. Which workloads are suspended/reduced?
   The lowest-priority QM CPU consumers first (Connected Apps, OTA, Media, Nav).
   ASIL apps are never suspended.
3. How do you maintain safety?
   The ASIL reserve + ordering ensure safety CPU demand is met before any QM
   allocation; safety_status remains NOMINAL as long as ASIL deadlines hold.

Scenario 4 — NPU failure occurs
1. How does the system recover?
   Modelled by dropping the NPU pool capacity; the scheduler re-allocates the
   reduced NPU among workloads in safety-priority order, protecting critical
   inference (AEB, fusion) first.
2. Is there a fallback mechanism?
   In this prototype, degraded-capacity handling: safety inference retains priority
   and QM inference is dropped. A production fallback would migrate inference to GPU/
   CPU — the abstract Compute-Unit model makes that reassignment natural.
3. What workload is migrated?
   Safety-critical inference (AEB/ACC/fusion) is preserved/migrated first; non-
   safety NPU users (voice, connected apps) are shed.


================================================================================
 15. PERFORMANCE
================================================================================

1. What performance improvements did you achieve?
   Versus static worst-case partitioning, dynamic pooling keeps utilisation high
   while guaranteeing zero ASIL deadline violations — demonstrated live as
   safety_status stays NOMINAL for ASIL even under emergency load.

2. How much latency reduction was observed?
   Safety apps run at base latency (service_ratio 1) instead of being blocked by
   QM contention; QM latency rises only under overload, exactly as intended.

3. How much resource utilisation improved?
   total_util (sum used / sum capacity) trends much higher than fixed partitioning
   because idle capacity is immediately reused — visible on the UtilizationChart.

4. How many workloads can be supported?
   12 in the demo across all domains; the catalog and platform are data-driven, so
   more can be added without code changes.

5. How many scheduling decisions per second?
   A full platform re-allocation every tick (1 Hz here); the algorithm is
   O(n log n) and can run far faster if the tick rate is increased.

6. What is the scheduler overhead?
   Minimal — a sort plus three linear passes over the workload list per tick; pure
   Python and effectively negligible for 12 workloads.

7. What is the memory footprint?
   Tiny: a handful of dataclasses per workload plus a 40-sample history per
   resource in the predictor; runs comfortably in an embedded footprint.

8. What is the response time?
   Per workload, latency_ms from the queueing model, bounded by its deadline_ms
   when served fully (e.g. AEB ~5 ms base).

9. How do you benchmark the system?
   By running scenarios (idle/city/highway/parking/emergency) and spikes and
   observing utilisation, deadline_misses, throttled_count and safety_status on the
   dashboard.

10. What metrics prove success?
    asil_all_met = True under all scenarios (no ASIL VIOLATION), high total_util,
    and QM-only throttling under overload.


================================================================================
 16. EDGE CASES
================================================================================

1. What happens if every application is high priority?
   The tie-break chain still totally orders them (ASIL weight, then priority, then
   deadline urgency), so allocation stays deterministic; safety-critical still wins.

2. What happens if resources are exhausted?
   Greedy allocation stops at capacity; safety demand is served first, QM is
   throttled, saturation alerts fire — graceful degradation, not collapse.

3. What if monitoring data is incorrect?
   Allocation uses the reported demand, but the independent asil_all_met check and
   CRITICAL alert (ASIL demand > capacity) catch impossible/inconsistent states.

4. What if the scheduler crashes?
   The FastAPI background loop is isolated; the last good snapshot is retained
   (_last_snapshot) and served, and uvicorn --reload restarts the process. In
   production a watchdog would fail the platform to a safe state.

5. What if a workload becomes unresponsive?
   Modelled by disabling it (enabled=False) -> zero demand; it simply drops out of
   allocation without affecting others.

6. What if all GPUs are occupied?
   GPU saturates -> QM GPU users throttled first; safety GPU users keep their
   reserved share; a saturation alert is raised.

7. What if prediction fails?
   Prediction is advisory only; allocation still runs on measured demand with full
   safety guarantees, so a predictor failure never causes a safety issue.

8. What if latency exceeds threshold?
   deadline_met flips False, deadline_misses increments, and safety_status becomes
   DEGRADED (QM) or VIOLATION (ASIL) with an alert.

9. What if safety partitioning fails?
   The system makes it observable: if ASIL demand can't be met, it emits
   "CRITICAL: ASIL demand exceeds capacity" and sets VIOLATION — the trigger for a
   real vehicle's fail-safe.

10. What is the fail-safe mechanism?
    Conceptually: keep the last safe allocation, protect the ASIL reserve, and
    surface VIOLATION/CRITICAL so the vehicle can enter a safe state (e.g. shed QM,
    degrade features). Safety apps are structurally guaranteed first claim on
    compute.

================================================================================
 TOP 15 MOST-LIKELY JUDGE QUESTIONS (CRISP ANSWERS)
================================================================================

1. What problem are you solving?
   Safely and efficiently sharing one centralised vehicle computer among mixed-
   criticality apps (ASIL + QM) so safety apps never miss deadlines while
   utilisation stays high.

2. Why can't static allocation work?
   It reserves worst-case slices that sit idle (wasteful) and can't shift capacity
   when a safety app suddenly needs more — so you over-provision AND still risk
   misses.

3. What is ASIL and QM?
   ASIL A-D = automotive safety integrity levels (D highest) requiring safety
   measures; QM = quality-managed, no safety requirement. Our AEB=D, infotainment=QM.

4. How do you guarantee ASIL workloads are protected?
   Strict ordering (ASIL weight x 10,000 dominates) + a 70% per-resource reserve
   band + per-tick asil_all_met verification, so QM can never starve ASIL.

5. How does the adaptive scheduler work?
   Each 1 Hz tick: measure demand, sort by effective priority (safety, priority,
   deadline urgency), greedily allocate CPU/GPU/NPU top-down, then derive service
   ratio/latency/deadline status.

6. How do you allocate CPU/GPU/NPU resources?
   Independent greedy allocation per pool (900/800/1200 CU) in the same safety-
   priority order; each app gets min(demand, remaining).

7. What happens during emergency braking?
   AEB demand jumps 2x; as ASIL-D it's served first within one tick, reclaiming
   compute from throttled QM apps — safety stays NOMINAL.

8. How do you prevent resource starvation?
   ASIL can't be starved (reserve+ordering); QM starvation is bounded, gets leftover
   capacity, and is surfaced via throttled_count.

9. What scheduling algorithm and why?
   Custom mixed-criticality static-priority greedy allocator with deadline-urgency
   tie-break and ASIL reserve — chosen for provable freedom-from-interference,
   determinism, and multi-resource support (vs RR/EDF/RMS/WFS which we evaluated).

10. How do you measure latency?
    latency_ms = base_latency_ms / service_ratio; deadline_met = latency <=
    deadline_ms.

11. How do you detect overload conditions?
    used >= capacity -> saturated flag + alert; plus AI forecast flags predicted_util
    > 0.95 before it happens.

12. What metrics are on the dashboard?
    Per-resource utilisation, ASIL/QM split, avg latency, deadline misses, throttled
    count, safety status, total utilisation, and predicted demand.

13. How does AI prediction help?
    Holt double-EWMA forecasts next-tick demand so the system warns of saturation
    early and can protect headroom pre-emptively; it's advisory, never overriding
    safety.

14. What makes you different from existing schedulers?
    Standard schedulers are criticality-blind and single-resource; ours enforces ISO
    26262 freedom-from-interference across CPU+GPU+NPU with graceful QM degradation
    and AI-assisted foresight.

15. How would this scale to a real SDV?
    Data-driven catalog/platform (add apps/resources freely), stateless fast
    scheduling, and a clean interface to bind onto a certified hypervisor/cgroups
    enforcement layer with OTA-updatable policies.
